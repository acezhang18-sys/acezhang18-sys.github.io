Conflict Resolution

Overview

I handle conflict as an operations problem: restore the room, keep the mission intact, and leave people able to work together after the heat drops. I do not treat every disagreement as a values crisis, and I do not let a single incident become the community’s product.

Core Strengths

  • Separate the public room from the private correction so rank and face survive the fix
  • Name the behavior, not the soul, then put the next task back in the right hands
  • Read the room, separate feeling from fact, and balance both so the agreement holds without throwing anyone under the bus
  • De-escalate without erasing the standard that caused the friction
  • Translate between civilian and high-accountability cultures so neither side feels mocked
  • Close the incident with a rule people can reuse, not with a speech about feelings

Relevant Experience

  • Pre-Selection — Staffed a mixed civilian and military-prep community where credibility fights, personality clashes, and “who owns the room” disputes show up in public. Practiced splitting staff decisions from general chat, restoring a counterpart’s authority after a hard call, and keeping the space usable after the argument.
  • 38th Tactical Division —Ran conflict inside a ranked cooperative unit: command chat versus floor chat, jokes that land wrong, and pressure between people who want to “say it straight” and people who need the room to stay playable. Resolved issues by role and standard, not by turning the unit into a tribunal.
  • Team Echoes — Routed design disagreements through an established procedure and crowd consensus: a “council of the experts” (Player Design Committee) so calls were made by process, neither by founder veto nor by a pile-on in public chat.

Method

  • Decide the audience first. Some truth belongs in the hut. Some belongs in the gravel.
  • Separate what people feel from what happened. Use both. Feeling tells you what will break the deal; fact tells you what the deal must say.
  • Pay a visible price if you own the room. Correction that costs the leader nothing trains contempt.
  • Restore with a task. Give the other person work in front of the same witnesses who saw the hit.
  • Keep the truth. Change who hears it and when.
  • End with an operating rule: who briefs whom, what happens next time, when the huddle breaks — and with no one used as the sacrifice that makes the others look clean.

What This Means for Clients / Collaborators

I can step into a group that is stuck on a person or a blow-up, put the fight back in the right room, protect the people who still have to work together, and leave a short rule so the same fight does not have to be invented twice.
